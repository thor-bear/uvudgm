# B-Roll Philosophy

## Definition
The course's working set of principles for how supplementary footage (B-Roll) should function in a cut — not as decoration, but as active storytelling material.

## In Practice
Laid out most fully in Lesson 11: B-Roll should support the story and lean on showing rather than telling; it shouldn't function as a bandage slapped over a jump cut; and cutaway shots read as more intentional in groups of roughly three, since constantly cutting back and forth between a single shot and an interview reads as less deliberate. First introduced in Lesson 6's discussion of "knowing your footage better than anyone" as an editor's core responsibility, and echoed again in Lesson 16 when reviewing the finished Gnarly documentaries — the emphasis there is specifically on B-Roll that builds character and advances story, explicitly *not* just impressive skating shots for their own sake.

## Teaching Analogies & Examples
- **"Show, don't tell" as a literal test**: if a line of dialogue says "I love skating," ask students whether a shot of someone actually skating would communicate that better than the sentence alone — if yes, that's the B-Roll opportunity.
- The "rule of three" for cutaways is a good rule of thumb to state plainly: one cutaway can feel like a mistake, two can feel like a coincidence, three starts to feel like a deliberate pattern the editor is building.
- Ask students to imagine muting a scene entirely — does the B-Roll alone still tell you what's happening and how to feel about it? If not, it's probably decorative rather than doing real storytelling work.
