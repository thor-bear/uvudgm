# Coverage

## Definition
The set of camera setups (Master, OTS, Medium, close-ups, etc.) shot to capture a single scene, giving the editor multiple angles and options to construct the final cut from.

## In Practice
Introduced in Lesson 17 as a new concept for most students: for a one-page scene, in most cases every setup used covers the full scene, not just a fragment of it. Uses the "Coverage Folder – Statues" photo set as a concrete example. The lesson has students guess *why* a **Master Shot** is always shot first before revealing the four reasons (blocking/rhythm, continuity insurance, pacing performer energy, and establishing lighting) — a deliberate "ask before telling" pattern the course reuses elsewhere (see the Three-Act Structure discussion in Lesson 8). Directly informs the Jacuzzi dialogue assignment: Lesson 19a has students watch each scene's Master and study coverage for pickups and performance changes take-to-take, and Lesson 25's Tom Cross interview discussion references the *Whiplash* slate reaching "CA" (Charlie Apple) — meaning coverage on that scene ran through the alphabet twice, cited as a striking illustration of how extensive coverage can get on a high-end production.

## Teaching Analogies & Examples
- **Photographing a room from every angle before you decorate it**: you don't know yet which photo you'll want to use for which purpose, so you shoot wide, medium, and close on everything, then decide in the edit.
- The "CA" (Charlie Apple) *Whiplash* detail is genuinely the best real-world hook for this concept — most students are shocked that coverage can run through the entire alphabet twice on a single scene, and it makes the abstract idea of "extensive coverage" concrete and memorable.
- Useful gut-check question for students reviewing their own footage: "if I only had the Master and nothing else, would this scene still basically work?" — coverage is everything beyond that baseline.
