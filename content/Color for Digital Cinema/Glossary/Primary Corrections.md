# Primary Corrections

## Definition
Global adjustments applied to the *entire* image — not a targeted selection — that build a balanced, realistic-looking image. In scope: Lift/Gamma/Gain, Curves, Contrast/Pivot, Saturation. Explicitly out of scope: HSL qualifiers, power windows, or anything else selective. The goal isn't style; it's matching what your eyes would perceive looking at the scene in person. Maps onto the general industry term "color correction," as distinct from "color grading" (secondaries/style).

## In Practice
The foundation everything else in this course builds on. After primaries, an image should be white balanced, color-contrasted, luma-contrasted, saturated (not over-saturated), and — the single most important item — consistent shot to shot. Shadow balance is non-negotiable, even in heavily stylized final grades (Blade Runner's orange cast keeps neutral shadows). Even intentionally dark/moody footage gets balanced normally during primaries, with the intended style applied afterward in a later "look" node — this keeps selections clean and controllable. Not treated as subjective: footage is either balanced or it isn't, matched or it isn't, unlike a [[Atlas/Notes/Student Notes/Color for Digital Cinema/Glossary/Look (Color Grade)]].

## Teaching Analogies & Examples
- **Tuning a piano before you play a song**: primaries aren't the performance (the style/look) — they're getting the instrument itself into correct, neutral working order first, so every note that follows actually lands true.
- The "sitcom look" framing already used elsewhere in this course is itself a great teaching anchor: describe it as "boring on purpose" — a correctly primaried shot should look almost unremarkable, because all the interesting decisions come later, on top of a clean foundation.
