# Look Matching via Scopes (Reference Footage Analysis)

## Definition
Importing reference footage from a film with a similar tone/style to a project, then reading its [[Atlas/Notes/Student Notes/Color for Digital Cinema/Glossary/Parade (Scope)]] (where highlights/shadows/midtones actually land) and [[Atlas/Notes/Student Notes/Color for Digital Cinema/Glossary/Vectorscope]] (saturation, dominant hues) to reverse-engineer what a professional colorist actually did numerically, not just impressionistically.

## In Practice
Recommended as an ongoing habit, not a one-off assignment: deliberately watch a film you admire *through the scopes* rather than just watching it. Sourcing high-quality reference footage matters — standard streaming trailers (e.g. YouTube) are described as too heavily compressed to analyze meaningfully; better sources named in this course include Apple Trailers, ShotDeck (a paid, searchable still-reference database), and ripping Blu-ray discs directly for genuinely high-bitrate material. A recurring principle when matching: you're copying the *style*, not the *environment* — a reference's production design and lighting setup will differ from your own footage, and that's fine; the goal is matching the grade's logic, not literally recreating the scene. Shadows staying neutral even in an extreme reference look is the single most repeated observation this technique surfaces.

## Teaching Analogies & Examples
- **Reverse-engineering a recipe by tasting the dish carefully**: rather than guessing at a reference look by eye, you put the reference image itself up on the scopes and read its actual numbers — where its shadows sit on the Parade, how saturated its skin tones read on the Vectorscope — then aim your own grade at those same measured targets instead of an impression.
